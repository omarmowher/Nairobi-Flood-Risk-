Section 01

Mapping flood susceptibility across all of Nairobi

Nairobi's topographic diversity — from the Ngong Hills escarpment in the west to the flat Athi plains in the east — means different sub-counties face fundamentally different flood conditions. This analysis produces a Flood Susceptibility Index (FSI) for every sub-county using a combination of physical terrain factors and rainfall-based evidence.

Ten conditioning factors were processed in QGIS: slope, elevation, topographic wetness index (TWI), drainage density, distance to rivers, distance to roads, surface curvature, NDVI, soil type, and mean annual rainfall. Each factor was classified and given a Frequency Ratio weight based on historical flood occurrence, then combined using AHP-derived factor weights.

Section 02

Key findings: the Embakasi cluster dominates high susceptibility

The eastern sub-counties — Embakasi Central, Embakasi West, Embakasi North, and Starehe — record the highest mean FSI scores, all above 4.0 on the 1–5 scale. Their flat terrain, high drainage density, and proximity to the Nairobi River and its tributaries drive elevated susceptibility.

12 of 17 sub-counties have a majority FSI class of "High" (class 4). The remaining five — Dagoretti, Kibra, Kilimani, Langata, and Westlands — sit in the "Moderate" class, partly owing to higher elevations and better natural drainage toward the Ngong Hills.

Section 03

Comparing FSI scores across all 17 sub-counties

Embakasi Central (4.32) and Embakasi West (4.27) top the ranking, followed closely by Embakasi North and Starehe. The five westernmost sub-counties — which benefit from the higher, better-drained terrain near the Ngong escarpment — record the lowest mean scores, all below 3.5.

Even the "Moderate" sub-counties carry mean FSI values above 2.8, reflecting Nairobi's city-wide exposure to flooding risk. There are no "Very Low" or "Low" sub-counties in this analysis — a finding that underscores the importance of city-wide preparedness rather than concentrating only on the highest-risk areas.

Section 05

The ten conditioning factors

Each factor captures a different physical control on flood occurrence. Together they explain how water accumulates, where it drains, and which surfaces are most vulnerable to inundation.

  • 1
    Slope — steeper slopes shed water quickly; flat areas accumulate it. Derived from the DEM.
  • 2
    Elevation — lower elevations receive drainage from upslope and are more prone to ponding.
  • 3
    Topographic Wetness Index (TWI) — combines slope and contributing area to model where soil moisture concentrates.
  • 4
    Drainage Density — higher stream density means more surface channels for water to overflow during intense rainfall.
  • 5
    Distance to Rivers — proximity to mapped channels increases inundation risk during river overtopping events.
  • 6
    Distance to Roads — roads impede drainage; areas near busy roads often experience ponding from blocked culverts.
  • 7
    Curvature — concave surfaces (negative curvature) collect overland flow; convex surfaces disperse it.
  • 8
    NDVI — vegetation cover reduces runoff via infiltration and interception; low NDVI (bare or paved) accelerates runoff.
  • 9
    Soil Type — clay-rich soils have low infiltration capacity and generate more runoff than sandy or loamy soils.
  • 10
    Rainfall — mean annual rainfall sets the background moisture regime; higher rainfall amplifies all other factors.
Section 06

Frequency Ratio — how each class is scored

The Frequency Ratio (FR) model compares the distribution of flood inventory points (documented flood locations) across each class of every conditioning factor with the distribution of that class in the total study area.

FR = (% of flood pixels in class) ÷ (% of total area in class). An FR > 1 means that class is over-represented in flood locations — it has a higher-than-expected association with flooding. FR = 1 is neutral. FR < 1 means the class is under-represented — it is less likely to flood than average.

Each pixel in the study area receives an FR score for each factor. The ten FR scores are then combined using the AHP weights to produce the final FSI raster. The FSI raster is classified into five equal-interval classes (1–5) to produce the final susceptibility map.

FR valueInterpretation
> 2.0Strong positive association with flooding
1.0 – 2.0Moderate positive association
= 1.0Neutral — same proportion as study area
0.5 – 1.0Below-average association
< 0.5Strong negative association (unlikely to flood)
Section 07

AHP weighting — ranking the factors by importance

The Analytical Hierarchy Process (AHP) assigns a relative weight to each of the ten factors based on expert pairwise comparisons. Factors deemed more influential in determining flood susceptibility receive higher weights in the final FSI calculation.

Each pair of factors is compared on a 1–9 scale: 1 means equally important; 9 means one factor is extremely more important than the other. The pairwise comparison matrix is then normalized to derive the weight of each factor. A Consistency Ratio (CR) below 0.10 confirms the judgements are internally consistent.

In this analysis, Rainfall, TWI, and Distance to Rivers received the highest AHP weights — reflecting expert judgement that moisture regime, terrain-driven water accumulation, and channel proximity are the primary controls on flood susceptibility in Nairobi's context.

The final FSI for each pixel is the weighted sum: FSI = Σ(FRi × Weighti) across all 10 factors. The weights sum to 1.0.

Section 08

OND 2026 rainfall outlook

Kenya Meteorological Department's October–December 2026 seasonal outlook projects above-average rainfall across Nairobi and the wider central highlands, with onset expected in the second or third week of October.

NOAA's Climate Prediction Center gives greater than 90% probability of a very strong El Niño event persisting through late 2026. El Niño years reliably enhance the short rains over East Africa. In 2019, an El Niño-enhanced short rains season displaced an estimated 25,000 people across Nairobi in a single rainy season.

The sub-counties flagged "High" in this FSI analysis — particularly the Embakasi cluster, Starehe, and Mathare — are precisely the ones documented as most impacted during past El Niño flood events. This convergence of modelled susceptibility and forecast conditions makes OND 2026 a high-priority preparedness window.

Section 09

Step-by-step methodology

The analysis was carried out entirely in QGIS using open spatial datasets. Each step builds on the previous.

  • 1
    Factor preparation — each of the 10 conditioning rasters was resampled to a common resolution and clipped to the Nairobi county boundary. All layers were projected to UTM Zone 37S.
  • 2
    Classification — each factor raster was reclassified into five classes using natural breaks or expert-defined thresholds appropriate to its distribution in Nairobi.
  • 3
    FR calculation — flood inventory points were overlaid on each classified factor raster. The FR value was computed for each class and used to replace class values in the raster.
  • 4
    AHP weighting — a pairwise comparison matrix was built for all 10 factors. Weights were derived, and CR was checked (< 0.10).
  • 5
    FSI raster — all 10 weighted FR rasters were summed using the raster calculator: FSI = Σ(FRi × wi). The output was classified 1–5.
  • 6
    Zonal statistics — the FSI raster was summarised per sub-county polygon to derive mean FSI, majority class, min, and max for this dashboard.
